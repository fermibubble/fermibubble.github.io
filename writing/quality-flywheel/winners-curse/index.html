The Winner's Curse of Iteration

The more candidates you try, the more opportunities you create for a lucky winner.

Imagine testing a hundred harmless prompt edits. Some results rise; others fall. Choose the largest rise and its evaluation looks impressive—even if the underlying edits do nothing useful. Selection has concentrated your attention on a favorable error.

There are two distinct problems here. A candidate can exploit a weak proxy, and it can look better by chance. More trials create more opportunities for both. Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization studies the first mechanism: optimizing a proxy can eventually degrade a separate reference reward. Its “gold” standard is another reward model in a controlled setup, so the result should not be mistaken for a direct measurement of every human preference. [17]

Noise makes the second problem tangible. On Randomness in Agentic Evals studies 60,000 SWE-Bench-Verified trajectories across three models and two scaffolds. Depending on the selected run, its single-run pass@1 estimates vary by 2.2–6.0 percentage points. Temperature zero does not eliminate the measured variation. That is evidence from coding agents, not a universal noise floor for every application. [18]

The solution is to give exploration and promotion different jobs. Explore quickly on development cases. Then ask the chosen candidate to survive a comparison designed for a decision.

Pair candidate and baseline on the same tasks under comparable conditions. Repeat trajectories when the decision depends on run-to-run behavior. Estimate uncertainty in the difference, preserving the grouping of repeated attempts within tasks. Adding Error Bars to Evals provides a framework for treating these comparisons as experiments rather than isolated leaderboard numbers. [19]

A two-point gain with an interval from a one-point loss to a five-point gain remains inconclusive for many release decisions. It does not prove equality. It also does not justify choosing the favorable end of the interval as the likely outcome.

Protect confirmation data from the search. Once the optimizer repeatedly receives a holdout's failures, that set starts functioning as development data. AI Agents That Matter highlights inadequate holdouts and the cost of benchmarking choices that reward complexity without measuring its practical value. Include latency, resource use, completion, and critical regressions in the decision. [20]

For Atlas, the best-looking prompt from the inner loop is a candidate, not a release. It must handle unfamiliar certificate incidents, preserve performance on unrelated incidents, and fit the response-time budget. A failure on any of these checks is useful information about what the search has learned.

THE POWERFUL IDEA

Discovery can be opportunistic. Confirmation must account for how the winner was selected.
