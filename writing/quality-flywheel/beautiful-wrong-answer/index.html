The Beautiful Wrong Answer

The dashboard celebrates. The user still has the problem.

Imagine an incident assistant called Atlas. A service is failing. Atlas reads a log, recommends a restart, and explains the recommendation in polished prose. Unfortunately, the certificate has expired. Restarting the service changes nothing.

The team adds an instruction: include an explanation, cite documentation, and end with a summary. Atlas's evaluation score rises. Its answers look more complete. The certificate remains expired.

This is a fictional example, but it exposes a real design problem. If the checklist awards one point for a correct diagnosis, one for a citation, and one for a summary, a beautifully presented wrong answer can beat a terse correct one. The optimizer has found an efficient route through the scoring system.

THE POWERFUL IDEA

A better optimizer makes the weaknesses of its evaluator more consequential.

There is now direct experimental evidence for this concern. ProRubric, released in September 2026, reports a 4B model whose rubric coverage rises from 26.6 to 32.1 after rubric-based reinforcement learning, while appropriateness against held-out physician criteria falls from 54.1 to 26.0. Its proposed aggregation improves the latter to 36.8: a partial recovery, still below the untrained baseline. These are model-judged benchmark results from a very recent preprint, not clinical outcomes or a universal effect size. [1]

The uncomfortable lesson is that improvement has two moving parts. We need a system capable of finding better behavior, and a measurement process capable of recognizing it. Making the first part faster while neglecting the second can accelerate the wrong thing.

This article follows Atlas through a better process. We will turn its failure into a testable hypothesis, give it a more useful intervention, and decide what evidence would justify shipping the change. Along the way, we will examine how current research is automating parts of that process—and where human judgment and independent tests still matter.

The central question is simple: what survives an experiment that makes the next experiment better? That is where a quality flywheel begins.
